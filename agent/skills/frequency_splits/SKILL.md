# Training frequency and splits

Use these facts as evidence context. Do not turn a source fact into an individualized prescription.

- Matching different muscles' recovery times by changing per-session volume can require cycling the higher-volume muscle through reduced-volume recovery phases more often. [k:0f907350]
- Very high per-session set volumes close to failure may cause enough damage to interfere with adaptation; the transcript gives around ten sets per session as an approximate point for many muscles. [k:18af6f5c]
- Training frequency for a muscle should be based on how long it takes that muscle to recover from a hard workout, rather than applying an arbitrary schedule uniformly. [k:28d02fe6]
- The example proposes eight biceps sets and four quad sets per session as a way to align their recovery at a shared frequency; these are illustrative amounts. [k:2beb5da6]
- Recovery rates vary between individuals, so general faster- and slower-healing muscle group categories are not universal. [k:36f228b3]
- A two-frequency versus four-frequency arrangement is presented as a practical way to group slower- and faster-recovering muscles. [k:5757e834]
- Assess recovery using whether soreness has disappeared and performance has returned to normal or above before repeating hard training for a muscle. [k:5aeb7bcb]
- Using the same per-session volume and frequency for muscles with different recovery times can mean training some before recovery or leaving opportunities to train others unused. [k:67089b20]
- An illustrative split is training quads twice weekly and biceps four times weekly, based on the example's differing recovery times. [k:75d40fd0]
- Holding a muscle's volume low to match another muscle's recovery schedule may limit its growth potential. [k:764e0844]
- Larger, stronger muscles positioned for substantial lengthening generally take longer to recover from a given training stimulus. [k:8565ddb0]
- Muscles can recover at different rates after comparable relative training volume, so a single weekly frequency may not suit all muscles equally. [k:a1116bd2]
- Training muscles at different frequencies can allow each to receive training suited to its recovery without requiring the same schedule for all muscles. [k:bc55920d]
- One approach to coordinating recovery at a shared frequency is to adjust per-session set volume differently across muscles. [k:be0e4142]
- A uniform frequency such as twice or three times weekly can still work well for all muscles; differing frequencies are described as a more complex option. [k:e7042293]
- In the example, six failure sets for biceps may allow recovery in about two or three days, while six failure sets for quads may take three or four days or longer. [k:ef663446]
- Do not try to fit a muscle into a planned frequency when its soreness or performance indicates it has not recovered. [k:fbce808e]
