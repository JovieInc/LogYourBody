# Exercise selection by muscle group

Use these facts as evidence context. Do not turn a source fact into an individualized prescription.

- A suspected beneficial partial range can be tested in a training block and retained if it improves the stimulus-to-fatigue ratio. [k:046e30d9]
- For hypertrophy training, full range of motion generally outperforms partial range of motion in most direct comparisons. [k:0b2b452b]
- Loading a muscle while it is stretched may provide hypertrophy stimulus beyond tension alone. [k:4cbd5bb0]
- Complementary exercises can cover both stretched and contracted positions when a partial range omits part of a muscle's movement. [k:585b78dc]
- Partial range of motion can be considered when it produces a better stimulus-to-fatigue tradeoff than full range of motion. [k:a42bbf34]
- If another muscle limits a full-range exercise before the target muscle approaches local failure, the target muscle may receive less stimulation. [k:b19f5168]
- Full range of motion is not inherently an injury risk when the movement stays within tolerable limits for connective tissues. [k:d4daf4fe]
- A full range of motion often makes exercise execution more standardized and progress easier to track. [k:daec1966]
- Mid-range partials can be difficult to track consistently. [k:e1490c04]
- For strength specificity, the range of motion can match the requirements of the target lift. [k:ef2f69da]
- A hypertrophy program can include partials selectively while keeping most exercises in full range of motion. [k:f363d7a9]
